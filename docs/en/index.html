The Death of a Firefighter

27 Firefighters Killed in a Decade: Two Systemic Gaps That Keep Burning

National Fire Agency yearbooks show that major fires fell sharply over the past decade compared with the decade before, yet the number of firefighters killed on the fireground has only grown.

The causes of firefighter deaths are complex. For this report we interviewed Dr. Chang Hsien-ho, along with Lin Chang-chih, secretary-general, and Cheng Shao-shu, deputy chair, of the Taiwan Firefighters' Rights Association, to look at fireground entry rules, factory safety, staffing and daily duties, and ask how to learn from past tragedies, reduce risk and save lives.

Fireground command should put firefighter safety first

October 3, 2019

Taichung Daya Factory FireFirefighters Sent Inside Despite No One Trapped

At 1:45 a.m., a fire broke out at an illegally built factory in Taichung’s Daya District. No one was trapped inside. Nevertheless, a fire lieutenant ordered two firefighters to enter with a thermal imaging camera to locate the source of the fire. Ten minutes later, the building became fully engulfed. Neither firefighter survived.

United Daily News published a report in its print edition the day after the fire.

消防員恐被控告瀆職

Dr. Chang Hsien-ho, now a tenured associate professor at Embry-Riddle Aeronautical University's Worldwide Campus, once wrote in United Daily News' Opinion column about a question a classmate asked him while he was studying in the United States in 2005. It has stayed with him ever since.

At the time, a fierce apartment fire was about to burn the building to the ground. No firefighters went inside; they only sprayed water from the perimeter to stop it spreading. In class the next day he asked a classmate why they had not gone in to attack the seat of the fire. The classmate shot back: "Nobody was trapped. Why send firefighters in to risk their lives just to put it out a little sooner?" He was struck by how different this was from Taiwan. Just months before he left for the US, a fire had broken out at an illegal electrical factory in Sanxia, then Taipei County. A firefighter went in alone to look for the fire's source in a building with no one trapped, and did not come out alive.

Dr. Chang points to the three incident priorities used in the United States:

Dr. Chang stresses that when the risk to firefighters is likely to rise, commanders must assess carefully rather than rushing to shrink the fire or protect property. This order of priorities should hold from training through to the fireground, and firefighters should not have to pay for management failures that could have been controlled. Since Taiwan passed the three firefighter-rights amendments in 2019, neglecting risk assessment can lead to serious casualties.

On the experience of front-line crews in recent years, Cheng Shao-shu shared the account of an anonymous firefighter, nicknamed Chao, who narrowly escaped a factory fire.

Years on, fireground risk assessment for firefighter safety has not fully changed even after the right-to-retreat amendment. But Cheng Shao-shu points to the 2022 Meifu warehouse fire, fought defensively, as a case where the risk assessment was more cautious.

Crews advanced slowly from outside. The fire burned for 21 days before it was out, and the stench hung over the whole neighbourhood. The commander withstood pressure from residents and local politicians, refusing to chase a quick knockdown and putting firefighters' lives first.

According to the National Fire Agency's 2025 yearbook, fires in factories and warehouses, the categories most prone to industrial safety incidents, nearly doubled over the past decade compared with the decade before. More fires of this kind mean firefighters enter high-risk environments more often, and this is the main reason factory-fire deaths have risen in the past ten years. Central and local government audits of high-risk factories have yet to show clear results, and regulations and inspections need to be enforced more rigorously.

Dr. Chang Hsien-ho stresses that factories should build their own safety management systems. The simplest way to tell whether a factory is an accident waiting to happen is to look at its office environment and overall management. Factories that end up in disasters are mostly cluttered and chaotic, cutting corners on safety details and neglecting occupational safety rules until catastrophe strikes.

Cheng Shao-shu, deputy chair of the firefighters' rights association, says that after the deadly Xinwu fire in 2015 and the Chin-Poon fire in 2018, Taoyuan now has more complete digital building data for its factories than other cities and counties. Even so, the data firefighters hold is only partial, and conditions on the scene remain full of uncertainty.

After the 2023 Launch Technologies fire claimed the lives of four firefighters, the Pingtung County Council began considering a third fire station in Pingtung City in early 2024 to address staffing shortages and the heavy demand for emergency medical services among the city’s 190,000 residents.

Councilors questioned whether the new Pingsan Fire Station would simply draw personnel from nearby stations—Pingtung, with 37 firefighters, and Gongguan, with 20. Without an increase in total staffing, opening another station could weaken each crew’s response capacity and stretch personnel even thinner.

In an interview in late 2025, Pingtung County Fire Department Director Li Pin-cheng said the new station would receive an additional 15 to 18 personnel. Whether those positions would be filled as promised, rather than through transfers from other stations, remained to be seen.

Use Data-Driven Analysis to Determine Fire Station Locations

Ray Hsienho Chang, Ph.D., who has closely observed operations at the Phoenix Fire Department in the United States, said that spreading personnel across small stations does not solve staffing shortages.

Fire and police services once belonged to the same system in Taiwan, so many early fire stations were located near police stations. A nearby police station may give residents a sense of neighborhood security, but having a fire station next door does not prevent fires. Even if a crew arrives quickly, insufficient staffing means it must still wait for reinforcements.

An additional station can help share the demand for emergency medical services, Chang said. But effective fire response ultimately depends on training and coordination with neighboring crews.

He favors concentrating more personnel at larger stations. Beyond staff assigned to ambulance services, safety inspections, and other regular duties, this arrangement allows more firefighters to be deployed at any given time. Building a cohesive response team should come first. Temporary mobile posts can then be established for major events, such as New Year’s celebrations, large concerts, or parades, according to the expected crowds.

Chang explained that locating fire stations requires a different approach from defining police jurisdictions. Geographic information systems (GIS) should be used to identify densely populated areas and calculate the coverage reachable within an eight-minute drive of each station, compared with five minutes for urban fire departments in the United States.

Putting too many stations in the same administrative district can leave each with too few personnel to conduct full-team emergency response training.

International Comparison of Firefighter Staffing and Shift Schedules

According to National Fire Agency figures released this July, Taiwan has 18,254 fire service personnel, against an authorized staffing level of 23,716. The reported shortfall is 6,326, or 26% of the total.

Taiwan has one firefighter for every 1,280 residents, still falling behind the average of fewer than 1,000 residents per firefighter in neighboring Asian countries.

NAFR vice chair Cheng Shao-shu urged the government to close this gap by working toward a ratio of one firefighter per 1,000 residents.

Too Many Duties, Not Enough Time: Can Smart Systems Help Firefighters Keep Up?

Cheng observed that many stations have just five firefighters on duty to maintain basic operations. Training is interrupted as soon as a call comes in. Over time, this makes it impossible to carry out intensive training for complex scenarios requiring coordinated action.

Since fire services became independent of the police system in 1995, some routine assignments have become increasingly difficult to justify. These include nighttime patrols around election campaign headquarters, vehicle patrols to prevent drowning, and door-to-door visits to promote smoke alarms.

Cheng said that non-emergency medical calls take up 70% to 80% of firefighters’ time. With too little time left for training, they must also perform duties resembling police work. When a fire breaks out, these assignments can make it harder to reach the scene promptly.

Given the staffing shortage, Chang said, Taiwan should reconsider whether a dedicated watch desk is necessary. Phasing out the requirement to staff it could free up at least one additional firefighter per shift.

This September brought a breakthrough in station staffing. Previously, stations assigned personnel to keep the watch desk staffed around the clock. The Taipei City Fire Department introduced an Internet of Things (IoT) system through its first “smart-duty fire station” initiative.

When an emergency call comes in, the system automatically broadcasts dispatch alerts to the floors where responding personnel are located, turns on lights, and opens vehicle bay doors. Automating these tasks frees up watch desk personnel and improves response efficiency.

The pilot has performed well so far, raising the prospect of expansion to other stations.

Advancing Firefighters’ Rights and Working Conditions

Training a firefighter takes considerable effort. Their lives are more precious still. From administrative management to frontline rescue operations, Taiwan must consistently put firefighters’ life safety first.

NAFR secretary-general Lin Chang-chih said that efforts to establish a union have continued to face obstacles in recent years. However, some cities and counties have made substantial progress on working hours, rest schedules, compensatory leave, and overtime pay. These changes provide compensation for long hours—and, more importantly, give firefighters more time to rest.

Chang recommended that, alongside investments in facilities and equipment and changes to work–rest schedules and public-facing duties, the government devote resources to studying the nature of firefighters’ work and improving training and operational readiness.

Fire service administration should also be updated to reflect Taiwan’s specific conditions. Used effectively, AI could support more accurate decision-making, so that emergency responders do not have to shoulder risks based solely on experience and judgments made at the scene.
